How to Price Your Freelance Services Without Undervaluing Yourself

Every freelancer has charged too little at least once. The fix isn't "charge more" — it's a pricing system. This guide walks through your minimum viable rate, value-based pricing, packaging, and the scripts to defend your number.

Step 1: Calculate Your Minimum Viable Rate (MVR)

Your MVR is the hourly equivalent you must earn to keep freelancing sustainable. Most freelancers skip this and guess. Here's the formula:

MVR = (Monthly expenses ÷ billable hours per month) × (1 + tax rate) × 2

The ×2 multiplier covers the hours you don't bill: sales calls, admin, invoicing, learning, and the gaps between projects. A worked example:

ItemAmount
Monthly expenses (rent, food, internet, software, health)INR 30,000
Billable hours per month (20 days × 4 hrs)80 hours
Tax + savings (30%)INR 39,000 total / 80 hrs
MVR≈ INR 975/hour → round to INR 1,000

If you're quoting below your MVR, you're subsidizing the client with your own savings. That's not "getting started" — it's a withdrawal from your runway.

Step 2: Switch from Hourly to Value Pricing

Hourly pricing caps your income and incentivizes slow work. The client isn't buying your hours — they're buying an outcome. A website isn't "30 hours of work", it's "more enquiries every month". When you price the outcome, the number goes up and the client is happier, because you're aligned with their results.

How to anchor value: before quoting, find out what the outcome is worth to them. Ask questions like:

  • "How much do you currently spend per month getting customers?"
  • "What would one extra enquiry per day be worth to your business?"
  • "What happened the last time you lost business to a competitor who showed up online?"

Then price against their number, not your clock.

Step 3: Price the Package, Not the Task

Never quote a single deliverable in isolation. Bundle. A "logo design" package that includes 3 concepts, 2 revision rounds, brand colors, and social media kit commands 3–5x the price of the lone logo — and protects you from endless revisions.

Packaging LevelExample: Website ProjectTypical Price (India)
Bare task"A 5-page website"INR 8,000–15,000
Standard package5 pages + mobile design + contact form + 2 revisions + training videoINR 25,000–50,000
Outcome packageEverything above + Google Business Profile optimization + SEO basics + 1 month of supportINR 60,000–120,000

Same work, three price points. The outcome package is barely more work for you — it's mostly checklists — but it's priced on the client's result, not your hours.

Step 4: Use 3 Tiers

Always present three options: a stripped basic, a mid package you want them to pick, and a premium. Anchoring psychology: the middle option becomes "reasonable" compared to premium. Name them Bronze / Silver / Gold:

  • Bronze: bare minimum, priced honestly as "get you started" — INR 15,000
  • Silver (your target): most popular, best value — INR 40,000
  • Gold: everything + priority support + extra deliverables — INR 75,000

Most clients pick Silver. If they pick Bronze, you still win — you just make less and spend less. If they pick Gold, celebrate.

Step 5: Quote in Writing, Confidently

Weak phrasing leaks uncertainty: "around 20k, maybe a bit more depending on..." Replace it with a clear one-page proposal:

  • The problem in their words (prove you listened)
  • The solution — deliverables, timeline, what's included
  • One price — fixed, no ranges
  • Payment terms — 50% advance for most projects; never start without it

Send it within 24 hours of the call. Momentum matters more than polish.

Step 6: Handle "That's Too Expensive"

Don't drop the price. Drop the scope. Script:

"I understand — that package includes [X, Y, Z]. We can start with just [core deliverable] at INR [lower number] and add the rest later. Which part of the scope would you like to cut?"

This keeps your rate intact and hands the trade-off to the client. If they push hard for a discount anyway, walk away — the cheapest client always costs the most in revisions and stress.

Step 7: Raise Prices on a Schedule

Raise your rates every time you have a proven case study, every time your calendar is above 70% booked, and at least once a year. Existing clients: raise 10–15% at contract renewal with a month's notice. New clients: quote the new number unapologetically — you're not a commodity.

The 3-quote rule: every time you quote a new price, get at least two more quotes out the door the same week. One rejection shouldn't shake your price — only data from 3+ conversations should.
